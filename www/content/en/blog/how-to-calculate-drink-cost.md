# How to calculate pour cost and cocktail cost

When you open a bar, the price of each drink usually comes from the competition's menu or a quick calculation before opening. Both fail for the same reason: they do not follow the real price of your bottles or how much you actually pour. This guide shows you how to cost a straight pour, a cocktail and a draft beer, and how to keep those numbers current.

## Step 1: get the cost per ounce of each ingredient

Take the latest invoice from each distributor and divide what you paid by the ounces you bought. A 750 ml bottle holds about 25.4 oz and a liter about 33.8 oz. For fruit, divide by the juice you actually get, not by the weight.

| Ingredient | Purchase | You paid | Cost per oz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blanco tequila | 750 ml bottle (25.4 oz) | $28.00 | $1.10 |
| Orange liqueur | 750 ml bottle (25.4 oz) | $15.00 | $0.59 |
| Lime juice | 1 lb of limes (about 8 oz of juice) | $2.00 | $0.25 |

## Step 2: cost the drink like a recipe

A cocktail is a recipe: every ingredient has an amount and a cost. Here is a margarita:

| Ingredient | Amount | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Blanco tequila | 2 oz | $2.20 |
| Orange liqueur | 1 oz | $0.59 |
| Lime juice | 1 oz | $0.25 |
| Salt rim and lime wheel | 1 | $0.15 |
| **Total per cocktail** | | **$3.19** |

Then add what usually gets forgotten: simple syrup, ice if you buy it, straws, napkins and any garnish.

A straight pour works the same way with a single ingredient: 1.5 oz of tequila costs **1.5 × $1.10 = $1.65**.

## Step 3: set the price with your target percentage

Decide what share of the price you want cost to be. If you choose 20%:

- **Margarita:** $3.19 ÷ 0.20 = **$15.95**, rounded to $16
- **Straight pour of tequila:** $1.65 ÷ 0.20 = **$8.25**

Then compare with your market. If the calculated price lands well above what people pay nearby, you can change the well brand, the pour size or the recipe before cutting the price.

## Step 4: draft beer, by the pint you actually sell

Draft beer works a little differently, because not everything in the keg reaches the glass. A half-barrel keg holds 15.5 gallons, or 1,984 oz: in theory, 124 pints of 16 oz. Between foam, the first pours and line cleaning, say you actually sell 112:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Half-barrel keg | $160.00 |
| Theoretical cost per pint (÷ 124) | $1.29 |
| Real cost per pint (÷ 112) | $1.43 |

If you choose a 25% target for beer, the price is **$1.43 ÷ 0.25 = $5.72**, which you might round to $5.75. With the theoretical $1.29 you would have landed at $5.16, losing margin on every pint without knowing it.

## The most common mistake: free pouring

Your cost is only as good as your pour. If the recipe says 2 oz of tequila but behind the bar it goes out at 2.5 oz:

- Tequila goes from $2.20 to **2.5 × $1.10 = $2.75**.
- The margarita goes from $3.19 to **$3.74**.
- At the same $16 price, your pour cost goes from 19.9% to **23.4%**.

That is $0.55 per cocktail. If you sell 100 margaritas a week, that is $55 a week walking out the door without anyone noticing. Using a jigger on every pour is the cheapest way to protect a bar's margin.

## Check your real pour cost for the month

Recipe cost tells you what your drinks *should* cost. To know what they really cost, count your beverage inventory at the start and end of the period:

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Beginning beverage inventory | $4,000 |
| + Purchases for the month | $6,000 |
| − Ending inventory | $3,500 |
| **= Cost of what went out** | **$6,500** |
| Beverage sales for the month | $30,000 |
| **Real pour cost** | **21.7%** |

If your recipes say 20% and the month says 21.7%, the gap is overpouring, spills, comps or drinks that were never rung up. It is worth tracking down.

## Keep it current

Bottle prices change. If tequila goes from $28.00 to $32.00 a bottle, the cost per ounce goes to about $1.26, the 2 oz pour costs $2.52 and the margarita goes from $3.19 to $3.51. At the same $16 price, your pour cost goes from 19.9% to 21.9%, and the same happens to every drink that uses that tequila.

To stay on top of it:

- **Check every invoice when it arrives** and compare the price per bottle with the previous order.
- **Find which drinks use the product that went up.** Your well spirit is probably in half the menu.
- **Decide quickly.** Adjust the price, the brand or the recipe, but do not let it ride for months.
