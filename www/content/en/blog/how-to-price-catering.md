# How to price catering per person

When you start catering, your per-person price usually comes from what competitors charge or what you charged last time. Both fail for the same reason: they don't track what your ingredients really cost, and in catering you quote weeks before you shop. This guide shows you how to build a quote from your costs and keep it current.

## Step 1: cost each dish per serving

Take your latest invoice from each supplier, work out the price per pound or per piece, and multiply it by the amount in each serving. Here's a lunch menu for 80 guests:

| Dish | Amount per guest | Unit price | Cost per guest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast | 6 oz | $3.36 / lb | $1.26 |
| Cream sauce | | | $0.40 |
| Rice | 3 oz | $0.64 / lb | $0.12 |
| Salad with dressing | | | $0.45 |
| Dinner roll | 1 | $0.25 each | $0.25 |
| Dessert | 1 serving | | $0.70 |
| Lemonade or iced tea | | | $0.20 |
| **Food per guest** | | | **$3.38** |

## Step 2: add the extra food

At every event you cook more than the head count: a guest who goes back for seconds, a dropped plate, someone who shows up without an RSVP. If you prepare 10% extra, for 80 guests you cook 88 servings:

**$3.38 × 88 servings = $297.44 of food**

Spread over the 80 paying guests: **$297.44 ÷ 80 = $3.72 per guest**.

This is the most common mistake: quoting from the cost of one perfect serving and forgetting that the leftovers were paid for too.

## Step 3: price the food with your target percentage

Decide what share of the food price should go to ingredients. If you pick 30%:

**Food price per guest = $3.72 ÷ 0.30 = $12.40**

For 80 guests: $12.40 × 80 = $992.00.

## Step 4: add everything that isn't food

Staff, disposables and delivery are charged separately or on top of the food price. Work out what each costs you and decide what to charge:

| Line | Your cost | What you charge |
|---|---|---|
| Food (80 guests) | $297.44 | $992.00 |
| Staff: 3 people × 6 hours | $324.00 ($18 / hour) | $540.00 ($30 / hour) |
| Disposables: plates, cutlery, napkins | $48.00 ($0.60 / guest) | $80.00 ($1.00 / guest) |
| Delivery | $40.00 | $60.00 |
| **Total** | **$709.44** | **$1,672.00** |

The final price per guest is **$1,672.00 ÷ 80 = $20.90**. The gap between what you charge and your direct costs, $1,672.00 − $709.44 = $962.56, has to cover your kitchen, rent, the hours you spent quoting and shopping, and your profit.

If you rent linens, chafing dishes or furniture, add them as another line. Many caterers ask for a deposit when the client books, often between 30% and 50%, to cover the shopping.

## Step 5: compare with your market

If your calculated price is well above what people in your area pay, cutting it isn't the only answer. You can offer a menu with a less expensive protein, change the dessert, adjust portions, or make waitstaff an optional add-on.

## Step 6: keep it current

In catering, the risk is time. If you quote today and the event is three weeks out, prices can change in between. If chicken breast goes from $3.36 to $3.84 a pound, the 6 oz serving goes from $1.26 to $1.44. Food cost per guest rises from $3.38 to $3.56, and for 88 servings it goes from $297.44 to $313.28: $15.84 less profit on a single event, from a single ingredient.

To stay on top of it:

- **Put an expiration date on every quote**, for example 15 days, and check your costs before you confirm.
- **Check invoices when they arrive** and compare prices with the previous order.
- **Recost the menu before every new quote** instead of reusing the per-guest price from the last event.
