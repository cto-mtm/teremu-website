# What numbers should a new restaurant track from day one?

When you open a restaurant, everything competes for your attention: staff, suppliers, the kitchen, guests. The numbers get pushed to later, and later is usually when the money has already run short. You do not need a full-time accountant to start. You need five numbers, and this guide shows you how to calculate each one with an example.

## 1. Plate cost

Plate cost is what you pay for the ingredients in one serving, compared against the menu price. Everything else builds on it.

Take a dish and multiply the amount of each ingredient by the price you paid on your latest invoice:

| Ingredient | Amount | Price | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chicken breast | 8 oz | $4.00 / lb | $2.00 |
| Rice (dry) | 3 oz | $0.07 / oz | $0.21 |
| Vegetables | 5 oz | $0.10 / oz | $0.50 |
| Oil and seasoning | | | $0.15 |
| **Plate cost** | | | **$2.86** |

If it sells for $10, its cost percentage is **$2.86 ÷ $10 = 28.6%**. Run this for your 10 best sellers first.

## 2. Weekly food cost

Weekly food cost tells you what share of your sales went to ingredients. The formula:

**Food cost % = (beginning inventory + purchases − ending inventory) ÷ sales × 100**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Beginning inventory (Monday) | $2,000 |
| Purchases this week | $4,500 |
| Ending inventory (Sunday) | $1,700 |
| **Ingredients used** | **$4,800** |
| Sales this week | $15,000 |
| **Food cost** | **32%** |

Many restaurants aim for a food cost of roughly 28% to 35%, depending on the concept. More important than the exact number is the trend: if it climbs week after week without a menu change, find out why.

## 3. Prime cost

Prime cost adds up food and labor, the two biggest expenses in a restaurant and the two you can control most.

Using the example above and a weekly payroll of $4,200:

**Prime cost = ($4,800 + $4,200) ÷ $15,000 = $9,000 ÷ $15,000 = 60%**

Many restaurants try to keep it around 60% to 65% of sales or lower. When it runs higher, what is left may not cover rent, utilities and profit.

## 4. Average check

Average check is what each guest spends, on average. Divide sales by the number of covers:

**Average check = $15,000 ÷ 600 covers = $25**

Use it to measure the effect of menu changes, server suggestions or promotions. If it drops, guests may be ordering the cheapest dishes or skipping drinks and desserts.

## 5. Break-even point

Break-even is how much you need to sell to stop losing money. First, split your costs:

- **Fixed**: rent, salaried staff, utilities, insurance, loan payments. Say $27,000 a month.
- **Variable**: costs that grow with sales, such as ingredients, packaging and card processing fees. Say 40% of sales.

**Break-even = $27,000 ÷ (1 − 0.40) = $27,000 ÷ 0.60 = $45,000 a month**

Over 30 days that is $1,500 a day, or 60 covers a day at a $25 average check. With that number in mind, you know every day whether you are ahead or behind.

## The most common mistake: confusing purchases with usage

Many new restaurants calculate food cost by dividing purchases alone by sales. In the example that gives $4,500 ÷ $15,000 = 30%, when the real figure is 32%. The difference is inventory: what you bought is not what you used. A week with a big order looks bad and the next one looks great, and neither is true.

If you cannot count all your inventory every week, at least count the most expensive items (proteins, dairy, alcohol) and do a full count once a month.

## How to keep the numbers current

Calculating these numbers once is not worth much; the value is in watching them move. If chicken breast goes from $4.00 to $4.50 a pound, the dish in the example goes from $2.86 to $3.11, and its cost from 28.6% to 31.1%. If you sell 40 a day, that is $10 a day less profit, about $300 a month, on a single dish.

To keep it from slipping by:

- **Check every invoice when it arrives.** Compare prices with the previous order and quantities with what was delivered.
- **Recost the affected dishes.** When one ingredient goes up, every dish that uses it changes.
- **Look at food cost every week, on the same day.** That way you compare like with like.
