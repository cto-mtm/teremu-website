# How to calculate food cost for a food truck

To cost a food truck menu item you need three numbers: the unit price of each ingredient, how much of it goes into the item, and the menu price. From those you get the cost per plate and your food cost percentage. This guide walks through it with a cheeseburger and fries, including the two things most new trucks miss: packaging and over-prep.

## Step 1: find the unit price of each ingredient

Pull the latest receipt from every place you shop and divide what you paid by the quantity you bought. Keep each ingredient in one unit: pounds, ounces or each.

| Ingredient | Purchase | Paid | Unit price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ground beef | 20 lb | $90.00 | $4.50 / lb |
| Burger buns | 48 buns | $19.20 | $0.40 / bun |
| Sliced cheese | 120 slices | $24.00 | $0.20 / slice |
| Tomatoes | 25 lb | $20.00 | $0.80 / lb |
| Frozen fries | 30 lb | $24.00 | $0.80 / lb |
| Fry oil | 35 lb jug | $35.00 | $1.00 / lb |
| Clamshell container | 200 | $36.00 | $0.18 each |

If you buy the same item in several places, use the price from your most recent purchase, not the cheapest one you remember.

## Step 2: cost the whole plate

Multiply the amount of each ingredient by its unit price and add them up. For a cheeseburger with fries:

| Ingredient | Amount | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Ground beef | 1/3 lb patty | $1.50 |
| Bun | 1 | $0.40 |
| Cheese | 2 slices | $0.40 |
| Tomato | 2 oz | $0.10 |
| Fries | 8 oz | $0.40 |
| Fry oil (estimated per plate) | 1 oz | $0.06 |
| Clamshell | 1 | $0.18 |
| **Total per plate** | | **$3.04** |

Add whatever your real recipe uses: lettuce, onion, sauces, ketchup packets, napkins. Each looks small, but together they can add several cents per plate.

## Step 3: find your food cost and set the price

Food cost percentage is the plate cost divided by the menu price. To set a price, run it backwards: pick what share of the price you want ingredients to be, and divide.

With a 30% target:

**Price = $3.04 ÷ 0.30 = $10.13**

Menu prices usually land on round numbers, so you'd choose between $10.00 (30.4% food cost) and $10.50 (**$3.04 ÷ $10.50 = 29.0%**).

Then check it against what people pay where you park. If the calculated price is well above the market, cutting it isn't the only answer: you can adjust the portion, swap an ingredient or find another supplier.

## The most common mistake: ignoring what you didn't sell

On a truck you prep before you know how much you'll sell. If you pattied 120 burgers for an event and sold 100, the 20 left over were still paid for:

**20 × $1.50 of beef = $30.00** that day, in beef alone.

That money doesn't show up in the per-plate cost from step 2, but it comes out of your margin. Two ways to keep an eye on it:

- **Compare purchases with sales.** If you bought beef for 600 burgers this week and sold 500, your real cost per burger sold is higher than the recipe says.
- **Track which events leave leftovers.** A few weeks of notes tell you how much to prep for each kind of event.

The second frequent mistake is skipping packaging. In the example, the clamshell is $0.18 of $3.04: about 6% of the plate cost.

## Step 4: keep it current

Beef and fry oil prices move often, and your menu board doesn't update itself. If ground beef goes from $4.50 to $5.10 per pound:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Beef per plate (1/3 lb) | $1.50 | $1.70 |
| Total plate cost | $3.04 | $3.24 |
| Food cost at $10.50 | 29.0% | 30.9% |

That's $0.20 more per plate. At 100 plates a day over 20 service days a month, it's **$400 a month** you stop making on a single item.

To stay on top of it:

- **Keep and check every receipt.** Compare each price with your previous purchase, especially protein and oil.
- **Know which items use the ingredient that went up.** The same beef might be in the burger, the tacos and the loaded fries.
- **Decide quickly.** Raise the price, adjust the portion or switch suppliers, but don't let it slide for months.
- **Keep event fees separate.** If an event charges a flat fee or a share of sales, that isn't food cost, but count it when deciding whether the event is worth it.
