# How much does a cup of coffee cost to make?

A cup of coffee costs the sum of four things: the coffee, the milk, the cup and the extras. With the prices on your invoices you can cost each drink in minutes. This guide walks through a drip coffee, a latte and an oat milk latte, and shows why drinks that look alike leave very different margins.

## Step 1: find the price per ounce

Pull your latest invoices and divide what you paid by the quantity you bought. Work coffee in ounces of weight and milk in fluid ounces, since that's how you'll measure drinks.

| Item | Purchase | Paid | Unit price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Espresso beans | 5 lb bag (80 oz) | $60.00 | $0.75 / oz |
| Whole milk | 1 gallon (128 fl oz) | $4.48 | $0.035 / fl oz |
| Oat milk | Half gallon (64 fl oz) | $4.48 | $0.07 / fl oz |
| 16 oz hot cup | Case of 1,000 | $100.00 | $0.10 each |
| Lid | Case of 1,000 | $50.00 | $0.05 each |
| Sleeve | Case of 1,000 | $30.00 | $0.03 each |

## Step 2: cost each drink

Multiply what each drink uses by the unit price and add it up. Most baristas dose espresso in grams; an 18 g double shot is about 0.64 oz. For sugar, napkins and stirrers, use an average per drink; here we estimate $0.03. For simplicity, the drip coffee uses the same dose of beans.

| Item | Drip / americano | Latte | Oat milk latte |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coffee (18 g, about 0.64 oz) | $0.48 | $0.48 | $0.48 |
| Milk (10 fl oz) | — | $0.35 | $0.70 |
| Cup, lid and sleeve | $0.18 | $0.18 | $0.18 |
| Sugar, napkin, stirrer | $0.03 | $0.03 | $0.03 |
| **Cost per drink** | **$0.69** | **$1.04** | **$1.39** |

The coffee is the same in all three. What moves the cost is the milk: in the oat milk latte, the milk costs more than the coffee.

## Step 3: find your cost percentage and set the price

Cost percentage is the drink cost divided by the menu price. Many coffee shops aim for drinks somewhere between 20% and 30%. To set a price, divide the cost by your target. At 25%:

**Latte: $1.04 ÷ 0.25 = $4.16**, which you might round to $4.50.

With rounded prices, your menu looks like this:

| Drink | Cost | Price | Cost as % of price |
|---|---|---|---|
| Drip / americano | $0.69 | $3.50 | 19.7% |
| Latte | $1.04 | $4.50 | 23.1% |
| Oat milk latte (+$0.50) | $1.39 | $5.00 | 27.8% |

Then compare with what people pay in your area. If your price lands well above the market, you can adjust cup size, milk amount or supplier before you cut the price.

## The most common mistake: pricing add-ons below their cost

The oat milk latte in the example costs $0.35 more than the regular one ($1.39 vs. $1.04). A $0.50 upcharge covers it with little to spare, and its cost percentage still rises from 23.1% to 27.8%. If you don't charge extra and sell it at $4.50, it lands at **$1.39 ÷ $4.50 = 30.9%**.

The same goes for extra shots, syrups and whipped cream: if the upcharge was set by feel, you may be giving away margin on every order.

The second mistake is costing the ideal recipe instead of what gets served. If your recipe says 18 g but the bar is pulling 20 g, the coffee in each drink costs about $0.53, not $0.48. Weigh a few real doses and use that number.

## Step 4: keep it current

Milk, beans and paper goods change price several times a year. If whole milk goes from $4.48 to $5.12 a gallon:

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Milk per latte (10 fl oz) | $0.35 | $0.40 |
| Latte cost | $1.04 | $1.09 |
| Cost as % of $4.50 | 23.1% | 24.2% |

That's $0.05 per latte. If you sell 150 whole-milk drinks a day, it's $7.50 a day, about **$225 a month** over 30 days.

To stay on top of it:

- **Check every invoice when it arrives.** Compare each price with the previous order, especially milk and beans.
- **Know which drinks use the item that went up.** Whole milk may be in half your menu.
- **Revisit your upcharges.** When a plant-based milk goes up, the upcharge you set months ago may no longer cover it.
- **Decide quickly.** Adjust the price, the size or the supplier, but don't let it slide for months.
