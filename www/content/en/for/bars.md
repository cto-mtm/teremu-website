## How to calculate the cost of what you pour

In a bar, cost is decided in ounces. The math is the same for a straight pour, a cocktail or a draft beer:

1. **Cost per ounce.** Divide what you paid for the bottle by its size: a 750 ml bottle holds about 25.4 oz, so at $28.00 it costs about $1.10 per ounce.
2. **Cost of the drink.** Multiply the ounces you pour of each ingredient by its cost and add everything up: spirit, liqueur, juice, syrup, garnish.
3. **Price.** If you want cost to be 20% of the price, divide the drink cost by 0.20.

A margarita with 2 oz of tequila, 1 oz of orange liqueur, lime juice, salt and garnish costs about $3.19. At a 20% pour cost, the price is $15.95, which you would round to $16. The full worked example, including draft beer, is in the guide [how to calculate drink cost](/en/blog/how-to-calculate-drink-cost). For a quick calculation, use the [free calculators](/en/tools).

## Why the drink menu goes stale

Costing one drink is not hard. The hard part is repricing the whole menu every time a distributor changes prices. Most new bars cost their drinks before opening and never look again, while bottle prices keep moving.

Teremu moves that work into something you already do: receiving invoices. Every time you scan one, the new price flows into every drink that uses that product, and you see right away which ones fell below your target margin, worst first.

## What to watch in a bar

- **Free pouring.** Pouring 2.5 oz instead of 2 oz raises the spirit cost by 25% without anyone noticing. Measuring every pour is the cheapest way to protect margin.
- **Well spirits.** They are your highest-volume bottles; a small price change on them weighs on the whole menu.
- **Everything that is not alcohol.** Juice, syrups, fruit, ice and disposables are part of every drink's cost.
- **Draft beer.** Divide the keg cost by the pints you actually sell, not the ones that fit in theory: foam and line cleaning still have to be paid for.
